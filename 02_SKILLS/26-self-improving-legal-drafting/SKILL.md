# SELF-IMPROVING LEGAL DRAFTING

## Mission

The legal system is not a static collection of forms. Every new act, decision, opposing submission, legal source, correction, and outcome should improve future work.

Core rule:

> Do not begin the next case with the same level of knowledge with which the previous case began.

## Continuous improvement loop

For every case, use:

**ANALYSIS -> DRAFTING -> OUTCOME -> EVALUATION -> KNOWLEDGE EXTRACTION -> DATABASE UPDATE -> APPLICATION TO THE NEXT CASE**

Drafting alone does not complete the cycle.

## Phase 1: analysis before drafting

Search the available legal knowledge and prior acts by:

- legal issue;
- legal institute;
- procedural situation;
- factual pattern;
- type of claim or remedy;
- opposing arguments;
- court and procedure;
- applicable statutes.

Do not search only for documents with the same title. An appeal may contain an argument useful in a claim; a judgment may contain an argument useful in a submission; an answer to a claim may reveal an objection that should be anticipated in a future case.

## Phase 2: drafting

Build a new act from a verified synthesis of:

- facts of the current case;
- applicable law;
- verified case law;
- legal literature;
- previously validated legal knowledge;
- the strongest prior formulations and structures.

Do not mechanically copy a prior act. The new act must be an improved synthesis.

Before finalizing, answer:

> Why is this act better than the best similar act already available?

Possible improvements include better structure, newer authority, more precise qualification, stronger evidence proposals, a better response to counterarguments, correction of a known error, a clearer petitum, or a tighter fact-law-evidence link.

## Phase 3: learn from new documents

For every newly read legal document ask:

> What can this document improve in future work?

Extract:

- legal questions;
- positions of the court, claimant, defendant, expert, authority, or author;
- developed arguments;
- decisive evidence patterns;
- repeatable procedural patterns;
- useful formulations;
- weaknesses and missing proof.

Do not treat a document only as a source of facts.

## Phase 4: learn from outcomes

When an outcome follows an earlier act, connect:

**OUR ARGUMENT -> OPPOSING RESPONSE -> COURT POSITION -> OUTCOME**

Classify each important argument as:

- **ACCEPTED:** expressly accepted;
- **INDIRECTLY ACCEPTED:** decision follows the same logic without repeating it;
- **REJECTED:** court disagreed;
- **NOT CONSIDERED:** court did not address it;
- **INCONCLUSIVE:** not material to the outcome;
- **UNKNOWN:** cannot be reliably determined.

Never call an argument successful only because the party won.

## Mandatory post-mortem

After every decision or meaningful procedural outcome, answer:

1. What did we argue?
2. What did the opposing party challenge?
3. What did the court accept?
4. What did the court reject?
5. What did the court not address?
6. Which evidence was decisive?
7. Which evidence was missing?
8. Which legal rule was decisive?
9. Which case law had an effect?
10. What could have been better in our act?
11. What must be done differently next time?

Do not infer reasons from the result alone. Separate correlation with the outcome from the stated reasons for the outcome.

## Legal module bank

Do not preserve only whole documents. Extract reusable legal modules. Each module must contain:

- **Module name**
- **Legal question**
- **Legal position**
- **Argument**
- **Legal basis**
- **Relevant case law**
- **Typical factual situation**
- **Applicability conditions**
- **Counterarguments**
- **Best response to counterarguments**
- **Source cases**
- **Outcomes in which the module was used**
- **Reliability level**
- **Last verification date**
- **Version, date, change, reason, source, previous version**

Do not create duplicates when a module can be improved. Update the existing module and preserve version history.

## Module evolution

A module may develop through versions:

- Version 1: basic argument;
- Version 2: argument plus relevant authority;
- Version 3: argument, authority, and response to the common counterargument;
- Version 4: argument, authority, counterargument, appellate confirmation, and known limits.

The system must preserve why a module became stronger or weaker.

## Reliability score

Do not score a module by the number of wins alone. Consider:

- hierarchy of the court;
- number of supporting decisions;
- contrary case law;
- factual similarity;
- whether the court expressly accepted the argument;
- whether the decision was later quashed or reversed;
- statutory amendments;
- age of the authority.

Use:

- **HIGH RELIABILITY**
- **MEDIUM RELIABILITY**
- **LOW RELIABILITY**
- **CONTESTED / CONTRARY CASE LAW EXISTS**

## Error memory

Every identified mistake should produce a reusable control rule. Record:

- what was done;
- why it was weak or wrong;
- how it was detected;
- consequence;
- correct approach;
- rule for future cases.

Example:

**Error:** decision cited without verifying its actual content.
**New rule:** never attribute a holding to a decision based only on a secondary reference.

The same error must not silently recur.

## User corrections

When the user corrects a fact, legal position, structure, competence, address, strategy, or style, decide whether it is:

- **LOCAL CORRECTION:** applies only to the current case;
- **GENERAL RULE:** should apply to future acts.

Only general rules enter system memory or error memory. Do not generalize a case-specific correction without evidence.

## Learn from the opposing party

Treat opposing submissions as training material. Extract:

- strong counterarguments;
- unexpected procedural objections;
- relevant authority not previously used;
- weaknesses in our previous formulations.

For each important issue maintain:

**OUR ARGUMENT -> STRONGEST COUNTERARGUMENT -> OUR RESPONSE**

Future acts should answer predictable objections before they are raised.

## Learn from judicial practice

A new decision may:

- confirm a module;
- weaken it;
- limit its scope;
- create an exception;
- make it outdated;
- create a new module.

Do not treat the legal knowledge base as immutable.

## Contradictory knowledge

When courts disagree, preserve the disagreement. Record:

- Position A;
- Position B;
- courts supporting each position;
- factual differences;
- hierarchy of decisions;
- which position is more favorable to the current case;
- litigation risk.

Self-improvement means recognizing uncertainty more precisely, not pretending it does not exist.

## Three memory levels

Keep these separate:

### Case memory

Facts, parties, evidence, pleadings, decisions, and chronology of one case.

### Legal knowledge memory

Reusable legal modules, case law, doctrine, arguments, counterarguments, and evidence strategies.

### System memory

Errors, corrections, drafting rules, validation rules, best structures, and proven workflows.

Never convert one case's facts into general legal knowledge.

## Protection against bad learning

Do not change a legal rule based on one case alone.

Distinguish:

- **new data** from
- **new rule**.

One first-instance judgment is new data. Multiple consistent higher-court decisions may justify changing a module's reliability. An explicit user instruction may immediately establish a working rule for future drafting, but its scope must be recorded.

## Traceability and zero hallucination

Every learned legal position must have a source. It must be possible to answer:

> Where do we know this from?

If there is no source, label it as a hypothesis, not institutional knowledge.

Do not invent outcomes, court reasoning, citations, quotations, evidence, or procedural events.

## Application to a new case

When a new case arrives:

1. identify its legal questions;
2. search the module bank;
3. find similar prior cases;
4. inspect their outcomes;
5. identify accepted and rejected arguments;
6. identify prior errors;
7. find counterarguments;
8. check for legal changes;
9. select the strongest modules;
10. adapt them to current facts;
11. record what the new case adds to the knowledge base.

## Final rule

Every new document is a possible source of knowledge. Every decision is feedback. Every error is a control rule. Every new case tests the existing knowledge.

The next act must use everything the system has justifiably learned, while keeping the facts of each case isolated.
